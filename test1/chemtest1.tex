\section*{Atomic structure and periodicity}

\subsection*{Average atomic mass}

Average atomic mass is the summation of each mass number multiplied by its relative abundance (\%).

\textbf{Example:} Neon has three isotopes: Ne--20 (19.992 amu, 90.48\%), Ne--21 (20.994 amu, 0.27\%), and Ne--22 (21.991 amu, 9.25\%).

To find the average atomic mass:

$$19.992 \times 0.9048 + 20.994 \times 0.0027 + 21.991 \times 0.0925 = \boxed{20.180 \lab{amu}}$$

\subsection*{Electron configuration and d-orbital stabilisation}

\textbf{d-orbital stabilisation} refers to the d-orbitals preferring to be half-filled or completely filled (5 or 10) rather than slightly underfilled or overfilled (4, 9).

The noble gas configuration of chromium, for example, appears to be [Ar]4s$^2$3d$^4$. However, to stabilise the d-orbital, one electron is borrowed from the $s$-orbital, making the final noble gas configuration [Ar]4s$^13$d$^5$. Similarly:

\begin{table}[H]
\begin{tabular}{l l}
Ag & [Kr]5s$^24$d$^9$ $\to$ [Kr]5s$^14$d$^{10}$ \\
Cu & [Ar]4s$^23$d$^9$ $\to$ [Ar]4s$^13$d$^{10}$ \\
Fe$^{2+}$ & [Ar]4s$^23$d$^6$ $\to$ [Ar]4s$^13$d$^5$ \\
Fe$^{3+}$ & [Ar]4s$^2$3d$^6$ $\to$ [Ar]3d$^5$
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
For ions, electrons are removed from the outermost level (4 for the two iron ions shown).

\subsection*{Periodic table trends and Coulomb's Law}

Coulomb's Law states that

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coulomb}
F \propto \frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}
\end{equation}

where $F$ is the force (repulsive if positive, attractive if negative), $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the respective charges, and $r$ is the distance between the two particles.

This becomes important when considering periodic table trends such as:

\begin{description}
\item[ionisation energy (IE)] energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion
\item[electron affinity (EA)] energy released with the addition of an electron to a gaseous ion or atom
\item[electronegativity (EN)] an atom's tendency to attract electrons toward itself
\end{description}

\textbf{EA and size increase down and to the left.}

In a given period, the greater the number of protons, the smaller the atom will be. However, as you go down the periodic table, the number of electron shells increases along with size. Electron affinity increases down and to the left, but its absolute value (since more energy released = more negative) increases up and to the right.

\textbf{IE and EN increase up and to the right.}

Ionisation energy increases as you go up, since the number of electron shells decreases, making the radius ($r^2$) smaller. Across the period, the number of protons increases, pulling the electron shell closer to it, making it harder to remove an electron. The higher positive charge pulling the electrons close to the nucleus is also the reason for the trend in electronegativity.

\subsection*{Multiple ionisation energies}

The first, second, third, and fourth ionisation energies of Al are as follows:

$I_1 = 580 \lab{kJ/mol}$\\
$I_2 = 1815 \lab{kJ/mol}$\\
$I_3 = 2740 \lab{kJ/mol}$\\
$I_4 = 11600 \lab{kJ/mol}$

The jump in ionisation energy from $I_3$ to $I_4$ can be attributed to Al's position on the periodic table; as it loses three electrons, it is stripped of its outer electron shell, giving it a full octet and making it harder to remove another electron from the new valence shell.

\subsection*{Energy effects in ionic compounds}

\textbf{Lattice energy} is the change in energy that takes place when separated gaseous ions pack together to form an ionic solid.

$$\text{Lattice energy} \propto \frac{q_1q_2}{r}$$

Between NaCl and KCl, Na and K have the same charge, but NaCl has the shorter internuclear distance (Na is smaller than K), so there is more energy released.

Between NaF and \ce{MgF2}, Mg is slightly smaller than Na, but it has a higher charge, so there is more energy released in \ce{MgF2}.

\subsection*{Hybridisation}

\begin{description}
\item[sigma ($\sigma$) bond] bond on axis between two nuclei (2 electrons)
\item[pi ($\pi$) bond] bond formed around sigma bond by 2 p-orbitals (2 electrons)
\end{description}

Each electron domain can have at most one sigma bond. All others are pi bonds.

\section*{Intermolecular forces}

\subsection*{Types of IMFs, in order of strength (weakest $\to$ strongest)}

\subsubsection*{London Dispersion Forces (LDFs)}

\begin{itemize}
\item In \textbf{all molecules}
\item Depends on \textbf{instantaneous dipoles} formed by random movement of electrons and induced dipoles, a temporary separation of positive and negative
\item The bigger the molecule, the greater the LDFs
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Dipole-dipole (dip-dip)}
Between \textbf{two polar molecules only.}

\subsubsection*{Hydrogen bonds (H-bonds)}
\begin{itemize}
\item Stronger version of dipole-dipole
\item Attraction of H in H--O, H--F, H--N to an electronegative nucleus (at least 1 molecule needs to have this bond)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Ion-dipole}
\begin{itemize}
\item Between an \textbf{ionic compound and a polar molecule}
\item \textbf{In order for an ionic compound to be soluble, ion-dipole force must overcome IMFs of solvent and ionic attraction}
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Physical effects of IMFs}

IMFs influence solubility, melting point, boiling point, viscosity, and surface tension.

\textbf{Solubility} depends on whether the IMFs between the solute particles and the solvent particles overcome the IMFs between the solute particles and themselves, as well as the solvent particles and themselves.

\textbf{Boiling point and melting point} depend on the strength of the IMFs. If the IMFs are stronger, the boiling and melting points will both be elevated.

\subsection*{IMF-based separation techniques}

\textbf{Chromatography} separates components of the analyte based on compounds' relative attraction to the stationary phase vs. the mobile phase.

There are three parts to chromatography---the \textbf{mobile phase}, \textbf{stationary phase}, and the \textbf{analyte}. The stationary phase is the fixed medium (e.g. paper), the mobile phase is the moving fluid (e.g. acetone), and the \textbf{analyte} is the sample moving along the stationary phase (e.g. ink).

\textbf{How much an analyte moves depends on the IMFs formed with both the paper and the solvent.} If the analyte is insoluble in the solvent, it will not move; the more soluble it is, the higher it will move.

\textbf{Distillation} separates compounds based on boiling points.

\subsection*{Properties of solutions}

\textbf{Vapour pressure} is the pressure above a solution or a liquid exerted by the solution or liquid. When \textbf{vapour pressure = atmospheric pressure}, the solution has reached its boiling point. Higher IMFs lead to a lower vapor pressure.

When a solute is added to a compound, it causes \textbf{boiling point elevation} and \textbf{freezing point depression.} This is because the solute disrupts the formation of the lattice structure, lowering the freezing point, and adding a solute to a liquid lowers its vapour pressure, increasing its boiling point.

\subsection*{Deviation from Ideal Gas Law behaviours}

The particles of ideal gasses have \textbf{no volume} and \textbf{no IMFs}. As the volume of the particles and the IMFs increase, the deviation increases.

At very high pressures, this deviation becomes significant.

Gas Laws:

\begin{gather*}
\text{pressure} \propto \frac{1}{\text{volume}} \\
\text{pressure} \propto \text{temperature} \\
\text{volume} \propto \text{temperature}
\end{gather*}

\subsection*{Kinetic molecular theory of gasses}

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ke}
KE = \frac{1}{2} mv^2
\end{equation}

At pressure = 1 atm, we can postulate:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Particles are so far apart that the volume is negligible
\item Particles are in constant motion, and pressure is a result of collisions with the container
\item Particles do not exert force on each other (no IMFs)
\item Average KE $\propto$ temp in K
\end{enumerate}

Every gas has the same average KE at a given temperature but different velocities.

$$V \propto \sqrt{\text{mass}}$$

\subsection*{Empirical and molecular formulae}

The empirical formula is the ratio between molecules in a compound.

\textbf{Example:} A compound is 43.45\% phosphorus, 56.36\% oxygen, and has a mass of 283.88 g/mol. What are its empirical and molecular formulae?

Assuming 100g:

$$43.46 \lab{g} \cdot \frac{1 \lab{mol}}{30.96 \lab{g}} = 1.403 \lab{mol P}$$
$$56.36 \lab{g} \cdot \frac{1 \lab{mol}}{16 \lab{g}} = 3.5225 \lab{mol O}$$

1.403/1.403 = 1 mol P, 3.5225/1.403 = 2.5 mol O

Multiply by 2 to eliminate the decimal---2 mol P, 5 mol O

\fbox{\ce{P2O5}} is the empirical formula.

To find the molecular formula, use the molar mass:

Molar mass of \ce{P2O5} = 141.94 g/mol

283.88/141.94 $\approx$ 2

\fbox{\ce{P4O10}} is the molecular formula.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:percentyield}
\text{percent yield} = \frac{\text{theoretical yield}}{\text{experimental yield}} \times 100\%
\end{equation}

\textbf{Example:} What is the max mass of \ce{MgSO4} $\cdot$ \ce{7H2O} formed if 100g \ce{MgCO3} is heated? If only 268g was produced, what was the percent yield?
